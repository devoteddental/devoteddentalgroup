Dental Crowns 101:
What You Need to Know

When a filling isn't enough, a crown steps in. Here's how dentists decide, and what actually happens during the procedure.

A dental crown is essentially a custom-made cap that fits over a damaged tooth, restoring its shape, strength, and function. It's one of the most common restorative procedures in dentistry, and also one of the most misunderstood, plenty of patients aren't sure why a filling won't do the job instead.

When a Crown Is the Right Call

  • A tooth is cracked or fractured badly enough that a filling wouldn't hold up under normal chewing force
  • Decay is too extensive for a filling to restore the tooth without weakening it further
  • A tooth has had a root canal, which leaves it more brittle and prone to fracture without reinforcement
  • A large existing filling is failing and needs more structural support than a replacement filling could offer
  • Purely cosmetic cases, like reshaping a badly discolored or misshapen tooth

The Difference Between a Filling and a Crown

Think of it as a matter of how much healthy tooth structure is left. A filling fills in a cavity or small area of damage while leaving most of the natural tooth intact. A crown covers the entire visible portion of the tooth, essentially becoming the new outer surface. When there isn't enough healthy tooth left to support a filling, or the tooth needs extra reinforcement to survive normal biting force, a crown is the more durable, appropriate fix.

Types of Crowns

Modern crowns are typically made from all-ceramic or porcelain materials that closely match the natural color and translucency of teeth, a major upgrade from older metal or metal-and-porcelain crowns. All-ceramic crowns are the most common choice today for both front and back teeth, offering a strong, natural-looking result. Your dentist will recommend the specific material based on the tooth's location and the amount of chewing force it needs to withstand.

What to Expect During Treatment

The tooth is numbed, then reshaped to make room for the crown to fit over it properly. From there, either a digital scan or physical impression captures the exact shape needed. Many practices now offer same-day crowns milled right in the office, while others send the case to an outside lab, which typically means wearing a temporary crown for a week or two until the permanent one is ready and bonded into place.

Caring for a new crownA properly placed crown should feel just like a natural tooth within a few days. Stick to your normal oral hygiene routine, brushing and flossing around it like any other tooth, and avoid chewing ice or extremely hard foods that can chip or crack even a well-made crown.

FAQ

Common Questions

1How long does a dental crown last?

With good oral hygiene and regular checkups, most crowns last 10 to 15 years, and many go well beyond that. How long yours lasts depends on the material, your bite, and habits like teeth grinding.

2Does getting a crown hurt?

The procedure itself is done under local anesthesia, so you shouldn't feel pain during it. Mild sensitivity for a few days afterward, especially to hot and cold, is normal and typically fades quickly.

3Can a crowned tooth still get a cavity?

Yes. A crown covers the visible tooth but the margin where it meets the gumline can still develop decay if oral hygiene isn't maintained. Regular brushing, flossing, and checkups are still essential even with a crown in place.
